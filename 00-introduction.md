# The Healthy Cast Iron Cookbook

## Simple, Fast, Wholesome Meals from One Pan

---

## Why Cast Iron

A single cast iron skillet is the most versatile tool in a healthy kitchen. It sears, sautés, braises, bakes, roasts, toasts, and even goes from stovetop to oven. It distributes heat evenly, retains it beautifully, and develops flavor through the deep browning (the Maillard reaction) that makes simple food taste rich. With proper care, one skillet outlives its owner.

For healthy cooking, cast iron has three quiet superpowers:

1. **Less oil, more flavor.** A well-seasoned pan needs only a film of fat. The crust does the flavor work.
2. **One pan, one cleanup.** Fewer dishes means cooking real food on a Tuesday becomes possible again.
3. **No coatings, no chemistry.** Just iron — the same surface humans have cooked on for centuries.

This book follows one principle: **cook real food, fast, with as few ingredients as possible.** Every recipe uses pantry staples shared across the book, so a single grocery run stocks weeks of meals.

## Caring for Your Skillet (the 90-Second Version)

**Seasoning** is a thin, polymerized layer of oil baked onto the iron. A well-seasoned pan looks black and matte, releases food cleanly, and resists rust.

**To wash:** While the pan is still warm (not hot), rinse with hot water and scrub with a stiff brush or chainmail scrubber. A drop of mild soap is fine — modern soap won't strip seasoning. Dry thoroughly on a low burner.

**To re-oil after each wash:** Rub a pea-sized drop of neutral oil (or olive oil) into the cooking surface with a paper towel until it looks dry, not slick.

**To rescue a rusty or sticky pan:** Scrub with kosher salt and oil, rinse, dry, then bake upside down at 450°F for one hour with a thin coat of oil. Repeat 2–3 times for a tired pan.

**Don't:** soak the pan, run it through the dishwasher, or store it wet. Don't deglaze with cold liquid into a screaming-hot pan (it can crack — though modern Lodge pans are quite tough).

**Do:** preheat slowly, use it often (use builds seasoning), and cook acidic foods (tomato, lemon, wine) for 30 minutes or less unless your pan is well-seasoned.

## A Note on Heat

Cast iron is **slow to heat and slow to cool.** Preheat for 3–5 minutes on medium before adding food. If it smokes, it's too hot. Most stovetop cooking lives between medium and medium-high. The pan does the work — resist the urge to crank.

For searing meat: heat the dry pan, add a thin film of oil, then the meat. Don't move it for 3–4 minutes. When it releases easily, it's ready to flip.

For eggs and delicate foods: lower heat, more fat. A well-seasoned pan handles eggs beautifully at medium-low.
